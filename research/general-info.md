## The Gold Rush: A Multicultural and International Event

When we think of the typical forty-niner, we usually think of an American — a young white man with European ancestry. And while thousands of these young men left their shops in Boston and Philadelphia and their farms in Ohio and Indiana to head for California, they were by no means the only group to come to California during the Gold Rush.

**Within the United States:**
- **Black Americans** — both free blacks from the North and slaves from the South. Though this was before the Civil War and the Emancipation Proclamation, there were large communities of free blacks in some cities of the North. Even so, their economic and educational opportunities were often very limited. The Gold Rush presented them with the possibility of a greater chance to succeed on the same level as whites. For slaves, the Gold Rush presented the possibility of freedom.
- **Women** — Most accounts dismiss women's presence, saying that except for a few saloon girls, women did not participate. Yet, though far fewer in number than men, women were in the Gold Rush from the beginning, with men and alone, participating in all aspects of it, including mining.

**International arrivals:**
- **Europeans and Australians** — Most like the Americans. By 1855, some 30,000 Europeans had come to California, mostly from the British Isles, Germany, and France.
- **Mexicans** — Coming northward first, mostly from Sonora, Sinaloa, Chihuahua, and Durango. Prospectors from Sonora were among the first to arrive in summer 1848, heading for the southern mining area and establishing the camp of Sonora. By 1849 others were coming from Peru, Argentina, and Chile.
- **Chinese** — Coming eastward from Asia. Numbers were small at first, but increased as word spread of opportunity in the gold fields and high-paying jobs in California. The Chinese settled in the larger camps throughout gold country, keeping to themselves and forming their own Chinatowns.

This was not a period of great understanding or tolerance for cultural differences. It was a period of American expansionism. At first foreigners were tolerated, but as more people flocked to the gold fields, hostility toward foreigners grew.

## Placer Mining — Separating the Gold

Although gold had been discovered in California by the early Native American and Mexican populations, the "Gold Rush" began in 1848 when gold was discovered by Marshall at Sutter's Mill in Coloma.

**Early years (1848–1850):**
- Gold was easily attained; yield was near 12 cents per pan
- A dedicated miner could make $20.00 daily

**Within two years:**
- Easily attained gold was depleted
- Good if color was found in five or six pans
- Wages dropped to $3.00 a day

### Mining Methods Compared

| Method | Efficiency | Daily Volume |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| Gold pan | Most precise (baseline) | ~1/2 cubic yard |
| Rocker box | 8x volume of pan | 2–4 cubic yards |
| Sluice / Long-tom | 1/2 efficient but 16x volume | 4–8 yards |

### Processing Gold from Sluices

After gold was collected in sluices, it had to be separated from accompanying debris. **Quicksilver (mercury)** was used for this purpose:
1. Mercury was deposited in sluices, mixing with gold and other materials to form an **amalgam**
2. The amalgam was taken to the "Pan House," where dirt was removed
3. The amalgam was processed in a **retort** — the quicksilver vaporizes, leaving gold behind (called "sponge")
4. The sponge was poured into graphite crucibles, heated, then poured into molds
5. A carbon deposit (from burning a pitchy stick) prevented the gold from sticking
6. The gold was shined with nitric acid and sent off to Nevada City

## Invention of Hydraulic Mining — Miller, Chabot, Matteson

In the early Spring of 1852, **Eli Miller** (a tinsmith), **Anthony Chabot** (an engineer), and **Edward Matteson** (a jack-of-all-trades) met in Sacramento and decided to come up to the gold fields. They wound up in an area known as "Deer Creek Dry Diggins" (Nevada City).

Once accomplished in ground sluicing, rocker boxes, and long toms, they went out on their own. Miller and Chabot founded a claim at Buck Eye Hill near Red Dog.

**The invention:**
- While waiting for rains, Chabot made a 100-foot, six-inch diameter hose from strips of saddlebag canvas
- Miller constructed a three-foot funnel
- This allowed them to bring water *to* the diggings — far better than taking excavated gravel to the water

Late in 1852 or early 1853, Matteson suggested attaching another funnel, turned around, to the discharge side of the hose. Miller fabricated a three-foot nozzle with an inch-and-a-half outlet — and **hydraulic mining was born**.

The system created more loose earth than the sluice could handle. Miller built a 200-foot long, 12-inch pipe in 12-foot slip-jointed sections to bring water to a distribution box (designed by Matteson) with two six-inch hoses. Caldwell backed the manufacture of these devices and had the same setup installed at his claim at American Hill, north of Nevada City. Because Matteson was seen bringing equipment to American Hill, **he was given the credit for the invention of Hydraulic Mining**.

**Economics:** Water cost was 75 cents per miner's inch ($153.00/week), but the three partners were capable of making **$50.00 per day per partner**.

### What Happened to the Inventors

**Matteson** also invented:
- A hydraulic derrick to move large boulders
- A hydraulic-powered steel bar platform for prying loose cemented materials
- A device for keeping debris from entering hydraulic intakes
- He failed to seek patent rights and **died a poor man in Nevada City in 1903**. His gravesite is still unknown.

**Chabot** left the gold fields in 1856. He:
- Created San Francisco's first regular water system (1858)
- Developed water systems for Portland, Maine and Milwaukee, Wisconsin
- Became an incorporator of Oakland's Gas Light Company (1866)
- Built the dam on San Leandro Creek (now Lake Chabot)
- Was largely responsible for water systems in Oakland and San Jose
- **Died a multi-millionaire in 1888**, leaving over $85,000 to Bay Area charities
- Chabot Observatory is named after him

## Water Supply System

The objective was to collect and store large quantities of water at an elevation considerably higher than the ground to be worked, then transport it to the mine via a closed pipeline.

**System components:**
- Main ditches following sinuous topographic contours
- Flumes and pipelines across steep slopes and ravines
- Intermediate reservoirs at the delivery end
- **Pressure boxes** — eliminated air bubbles, removed sediment, calmed turbulent water
- A large iron funnel at the bottom of the pressure box
- Main pipes of iron sheets, rolled and riveted, up to 2 feet in diameter
- Air valves at strategic points
- Lateral lines feeding the hydraulic nozzle

**The Bowman Ditch (major water supply for Malakoff):**
- Origin: Bowman Reservoir at headwaters of the Yuba River at Big Canyon Creek
- Length: 40 miles
- Dimensions: 5' wide at bottom, 8.65' wide at top, 3.5' deep
- Grade: 16 feet per mile
- Completed in 1869 by **800 Chinese and 300 whites**
- Water delivered to Waldron Reservoir for Malakoff's needs

## Drain Tunnels

### The Hiller Tunnel
Built between 1851–1856, financed by Dr. Hillerscheidt and D. Albert. Used for drainage at a small mining concern at the present site of Malakoff Diggins. Later bought by the North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company. Now used as a model of the larger tunnel below.

### The North Bloomfield Drain Tunnel
In 1866, Julius Poquillion attracted investors from San Francisco and developed the North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company. As the company expanded and bought the Bowman Ranch, they discovered they were using more water than the Hiller Tunnel could drain and would soon be working below its level. They decided to build a bigger tunnel down closer to bedrock, below the blue gravel layer which contained the most gold. **Hamilton Smith Jr.** was called upon.

**Construction:**
- Begun April or May 1872
- 8 shafts sunk at 1,000-foot intervals along the tunnel line
- Two crews went to the bottom of each shaft and dug in opposite directions
- 15 crews digging simultaneously
- Completed **November 15, 1874** — estimated one year sooner than if dug straight through

**Dimensions:**
- Mouth: 6.5 feet high, 6 feet wide
- From Shaft 6 to Shaft 8: 8 feet square
- Starting point in Diggins: 75 feet below the gold-bearing gravel of the ancient tertiary river channel
- At Humbug Canyon exit: nearly 400 feet beneath the channel

**Operations:**
- Debris flowed at a 4% grade through the tunnel into Humbug Canyon
- Moved one mile further along "under currents" before dumping into the South Fork of the Yuba River
- Workers paid $3.50–$6.00 per eight-hour day

Though the tunnel and its eight shafts are now filled with debris and water, it is still considered one of the **engineering feats of all time**.

## North Bloomfield Beginnings

As legend has it, in 1851 or 1852, a prospecting party of two Irishmen and a German discovered a rich deposit of gravel on the north bank of a creek, about 15 miles from Nevada City and three miles up from the South Yuba. When one Irishman went to Nevada City for supplies, he attracted a following of more than 100 eager miners who tracked him back. When the miners arrived, a tent city arose. The pickings were disappointing, and the disgruntled miners labeled the area a **"Humbug"** — meaning a lousy claim. Many returned to Nevada City, but a few remained.

After several years, residents submitted "Humbug" as their town name for a post office, but the Postal Service refused (too many requests for that name). "Bloomfield" was submitted but another town in Sonoma County had that name. They finally settled on **"North Bloomfield"** in 1857.

**Origin of the name "Malakoff":** Not certain. Possibly brought by French miners — the French were involved in the Crimean War where a battle was fought at Fort Malakov in Russia, and the mine's cliffs may have reminded them of that area. Cornish miners may have named it after a rock abutment at Cornwall, England called Malakoff. Park historian Loni Patterson found one reference to "A. Malakoff and Company" in a historic newspaper article.

## North Bloomfield Buildings

### McKillican-Mobley General Store

The main room dates back to 1856. The store changed hands several times, eventually becoming the McKillican-Mobley store. Notable features:
- **Six dark gray columns** — painted gray so horseback riders could tie directly to them without staining
- **Bulletin boards** on the front for election notices, hunting regulations, theatrical presentations
- **Steel bars over windows** — original security system
- Night watchman slept overnight in an 8x8-foot room; a large white pit bull provided additional security
- Also served as a gold exchange and gold shipment drop-off

**Goods sold:** Levis (Levi Strauss made the first Levis for Gold Rush miners), kerosene, ammunition, hardware, mining supplies, stationery, soap, kitchen utensils, paint, medications, eggs, oranges, bakery goods, canned meat, deviled ham, dried foods. Vegetables were NOT sold — available at the China gardens. Fresh meat at the butcher shop across the street.

**Gold** was the normal means of exchange. Merchants had scales for transaction purposes.

The store was also an effective social center. After work, miners congregated around the stove to exchange gossip. Those not in the temperance movement adjourned to the basement where two large barrels of whiskey stood — miners could serve themselves free. Ladies met at the post office to exchange gossip.

Five additional employees: a teamster (4-, 6-, and 8-horse teams), a local delivery driver, a full-time mail clerk, a stock boy, and a young woman for postal clerical work.

The daily stage's arrival was one of the most exciting moments of the day. The store **officially closed in 1941**, with Noni Landsburg as the last postmaster.

### Smith-Knotwell Drugstore

**Adrian A. Smith** (A.A. Smith) came to California by way of the Panama Canal in 1856. He was a member of the State Assembly (1863–64), a school teacher in North Bloomfield (1869), and Justice of the Peace (1873–1889).

A.A. Smith purchased the corner lot on Relief Hill and North Bloomfield Road in 1872 and built the drugstore building in 1876. The top meeting hall was used by the Independent Order of Good Templars, the Knights of Pythias, and later the Masons.

**John Knotwell** worked with A.A. Smith as a druggist from around 1876. He married Nettie Smith, A.A. Smith's daughter, on July 20, 1881. The drugstore became known as the Smith-Knotwell Drugstore when A.A. Smith moved from town and left Nettie and John in charge.

From 1900 to 1908 it was known as the North Bloomfield Drug Company. The current building is a reconstruction completed by the Masonic Lodge, E Clampus Vitus, and the State of California between 1976–1984.

### North Bloomfield School

The first school was built in 1857. The school standing today was most likely constructed in 1873 at a cost of $3,700, complete with furnishings and its own library. Classes were held for kindergarten through eighth grades, all in the same room with two or three students per desk. As many as **144 students** took lessons from two teachers the first year.

Principal F.D. McAlister earned $90.00/month; his assistant, Mrs. McDonald, earned $50.00/month.

The school served as a church and Sunday school on weekends (Catholic, Episcopalian, and Methodist services), and as a town meeting hall before Cummins Hall was constructed.

**Notable features:**
- Numerous blackboards — the primary teaching aid (paper was scarce during the Gold Rush)
- Children used slate boards and chalk for homework
- Bench at the front — for disciplined children
- Two cloak rooms at the entrance
- Large ceiling hooks to support the stovepipe for heating
- A ventilator in the center of the ceiling controllable from the teacher's desk
- Lit by kerosene lamps
- Separate "four holer" pit toilets for boys and girls behind the school
- A small bell tower and cupola on the roof

Behind the building: a depression in the earth was a pond that the school kids swam in, much to the principal's dislike. The pond was part of the LeDu hydraulic mine.

The school was last used in 1941 and has been owned and maintained by the Department of Parks and Recreation since 1973.

### Saint Columciles Catholic Church

Constructed in 1860 in Birchville, 15 miles east of North Bloomfield. Originally served as a Union Guard Hall where Civil War recruits were trained, then converted to a Catholic Church. St. Columciles was a disciple of St. Patrick who founded a monastery on an island off the coast of Scotland.

The church last held services in the 1950s. It was about to be destroyed when Bart "Babe" Pinaglia, a North San Juan businessman known affectionately as "Mayor," "Santa," and "Mr. San Juan," bought the chapel and donated it to the State of California in 1969. The church was moved to its present site in 1971.

---

### Skidmore House

Built circa 1862 by Rush Dix Skidmore ("Skid"), born in Versailles, New York, November 23, 1832. He came to North Bloomfield in 1857 and took over a bakery business. A hand injury forced him from baking, so he turned to the saloon business in 1860.

Skidmore married Elizabeth Plitch (a native of Germany) on November 1, 1861. They had three daughters and two sons. He was one of the wealthier citizens in town: notary public, agent for the Eureka and California Stage Lines, mining recorder, and philanthropist who often grubstaked miners less fortunate than himself. He was always dressed up — always wore a suit and tie — and was quite a history buff.

*"The girls were the apple of R.D. Skidmore's eye and no expense was spared in their attire or education."* — Isabel Heffelfinger, resident of North Bloomfield in the early 1900s

Mr. Skidmore died at his home on August 2, 1911.

**House layout:** Sitting room/office (mining transactions) → Parlor (entertaining guests) → Master Bedroom (left) → Child's Bedroom/Daughter (right) → Kitchen at the end of the hall

---

### Kings Saloon

The original structure was built in 1860 as an express office. Jack King remodeled it as a saloon between 1873–1875. King was a large, active man who often took matters into his own hands when drunks became obnoxious. The saloon was believed to be one of seven (perhaps nine) saloons in North Bloomfield during its heyday.

**Beverages:**
- Beer: 5 cents a glass — the most popular drink. Kept in the basement (no refrigeration). Locally made beer served from 5, 10, 15, and 25-gallon barrels.
- Imported beer: contained in ceramic bottles, a bit expensive.
- Hard liquor: had to be imported from Sacramento or San Francisco. A half-pint of whiskey might cost $2.00 — near the average miner's daily wage.

**Women:** There were few women in town. Saloon owners discovered they could attract business by paying women to be present and converse with miners — $20.00 to $40.00 a day for this service. If a miner could play piano, he earned extra income in tips and free drinks.

The Kings Saloon in the park today is **not an original building**. It was reconstructed in 1974 based on photographs of the original, by State Park employees and members of E Clampus Vitus Chapter 10 of Nevada City.

## Malakoff Diggins Statistics

### North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company
- 1,500 acres total; 1,200 of which was mining ground
- 148,480,000 cubic yards of auriferous dirt and gravel
- Producing capacity of $69,600,000 (at 20 cents per cubic yard)
- 80% of the gold is still present
- Malakoff operation excavated 41 million cubic yards of gravel between 1866–1884
- Yielded $3.5 million in gold at $17 per ounce
- Hydraulic mining contributed approximately **1/4 of California's gold yield**
- Gold was distributed in gravel on the average of **one part in 12 million**

### Malakoff Mine Pit
- 6,800 feet long; 3,800 feet wide; 600 feet deep

### North Bloomfield Tunnel
- 7,878 feet long; 6.5 feet high; 6 feet wide
- Grade: 4.5 feet per 100
- Shafts average 197 feet deep
- Situated 200 feet below the Hiller Tunnel

### Bowman Reservoir
- Completed 1876
- 96.5 feet high
- Stored 930 million cubic feet (7.5 billion gallons) of water
- Cost $151,512.44 to build

### Distribution System
- 43 miles of ditches, flumes, etc.
- Cost $700,000
- Employed 800 Chinese and 300 whites in 1868 to build
- Every foot drop of water in an enclosed pipe creates approximately 1/2 pound of pressure

### Hydraulic Monitors
- 500 PSI; 16,000 GPM; 1 million gallons per hour
- Used 16 billion gallons per year
- Weighed approximately 1.5 tons (Hendy Giant)
- As many as eight in operation at once
- Washed 50,000 tons of gravel daily

## Timeline: 1850–1930

### 1850–1859
- **1850** — Gold Rush had run its course. Wages down to $3.00/day. California admitted as a free state.
- **1851** — Gold discovered in Humbug Creek. North Bloomfield settlement begins as "Humbug." Population jumps to 300 almost overnight.
- **1852** — Hydraulicking invented by Matteson, Miller, and Chabot.
- **1853** — Sheet-iron pipe introduced by R.R. Craig on American Hill in Nevada City. Franklin Pierce elected 14th president. Lafayette Hotel construction begins.
- **1854** — Kansas and Nebraska become U.S. territories. Britain and France declare war on Russia (the Crimean War).
- **1855** — Hotel de France, second hotel in North Bloomfield, was built. French forces take the Malakov Tower in the Crimean War.
- **1856** — San Francisco firm begins manufacturing wrought iron pipe for hydraulic mining. County Court House burns July 19, destroying all records. As early as 1856, farmers in Marysville complain of debris in rivers, but build levees instead.
- **1857** — First postmaster of North Bloomfield. First public schoolhouse constructed. Rush Dix Skidmore moves into town. James Buchanan elected 15th U.S. president.
- **1858** — North Bloomfield becomes official townsite, center of Bloomfield township.
- **1859** — "The Miners News" newspaper founded. Oregon becomes a U.S. state.

### 1860–1869
- **1860** — U.S. census: 784 inhabitants. Abraham Lincoln elected president. Tragedy strikes North Bloomfield — exhaustion of surface claims and drought causes exodus of miners to Nevada's silver mines. Julius Poquillion purchases placer mining claims at depression prices (eventually owns 1,535 acres).
- **1861** — Skidmore House built. Civil War begins. Humbug Flume (2,000 feet long) completed after four years of work.
- **1862** — Rush Dix Skidmore marries Elizabeth Plitch. Homestead Act. Union and Central Pacific Railways chartered.
- **1863** — Town population rapidly declining, nearly depopulated.
- **1864** — 13th Amendment passed, prohibiting slavery.
- **1865** — Lincoln assassinated April 14.
- **1866** — Poquillion formed the North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company. First year showed a profit. Alaska purchased for $7,200,000.
- **1868** — NBGMC purchased the Rudyard (English) Dam and Bowman Ranch, constructed first dam of Bowman Lake. Filed articles of incorporation with capital stock of $800,000.
- **1869** — Transcontinental Railway completed May 10. U.S. Grant elected 18th president. Wyoming first state for women's suffrage.

### 1870–1879
- **1870** — Hamilton Smith Jr. designed the Bowman Ditch (completed September 15). U.S. Census, North Bloomfield: 636.
- **1871** — During the next nine years, hydraulic mining produced $121 million — more than half the total gold yielded in California.
- **1872** — Hamilton Smith engineered the North Bloomfield Drain Tunnel.
- **1873** — New schoolhouse erected at $3,700. Kings Saloon moves into express office building.
- **1874** — Drain Tunnel completed November 17. North Bloomfield population: 500. In any given 18 months, enough material moved to fill the Erie Canal.
- **1875** — Yuba and Feather Rivers flood Marysville and Yuba City, serious loss of lives. Six years of continuous court battles begin.
- **1876** — Levees built around Marysville ("The Walled City"). North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company in full operation. Seven "CRAIG" monitors running 24 hours/day. Well-paid miner earned $2.50/day. First telephone sentence by Alexander Graham Bell.
- **1877** — Rutherford Hayes elected 19th president. Phonograph patented by Edison.
- **1878** — Anti-Debris Association formed; petitions submitted to legislature. Survey by state engineers shows 18,000 acres of once-fertile farmland buried by mining debris.
- **1879** — Edison invents the incandescent light. Murder of banker William F. Cummings during stagecoach robbery.

### 1880–1889
- **1880** — Electric lights installed in the mine powered by a hydraulic generator. Malakoff using 160 million gallons of water in a 24-hour period.
- **1881** — Hydraulic mines along Yuba and Feather Rivers ordered to shut down temporarily by the court.
- **1882** — "Woodruff vs. North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company." Judge Sawyer states, "Debris in rivers is from ALL hydraulic mines, not just one." Debris dams already overflowing.
- **1883** — English Dam (first reservoir purchased by NBGMC) inspected and found in excellent shape — then broke three days later, releasing millions of acre feet of water. Sabotage suspected but never proven.
- **1884** — **January 7: Sawyer Decision** — imposed strict laws regarding debris, closed all loopholes. All tailings must stop. Decision was 225 pages long. NBGMC found in contempt; fined heavily.
- **1886** — NBGMC installed an elevator system to pull debris from tailings into holding ponds. This satisfied the Sawyer Decision but greatly reduced production.

### 1890–1930
- **1890** — Remaining hydraulic mines operating in accordance with Sawyer.
- **1891** — Congress passes the "Caminetti Law" — all hydraulic mines must be licensed.
- **1896** — U.S. District Court declares mining without permit illegal. NBGMC again found in contempt. Malakoff closes in the late 1890s.
- **1910** — NBGMC unincorporated. Landsburg and locals "bootleg operated" the mine occasionally 1910–1930 without ownership or authorization.
- **1911** — Rush Dix Skidmore dies on August 2.

## "The Elephant"

> *"I think that I may without vanity affirm that I have 'seen the elephant.'"*
> — Louisa Clapp

To forty-niners and those following, no expression characterized the California gold rush more than "seeing the elephant." Those planning to travel west announced they were "going to see the elephant." Those turning back claimed they had seen the "elephant's tracks" or the "elephant's tail."

**The origin:** A farmer hearing that a circus was in town loaded his wagon with vegetables. He'd never seen an elephant and very much wished to. On the way he encountered the circus parade, led by an elephant. The farmer was thrilled. His horses, however, were terrified — bolting, they overturned the wagon and ruined the vegetables. "I don't give a hang," the farmer said, "for I have seen the elephant."

For gold rushers, the elephant symbolized both the high cost of their endeavor — the myriad possibilities for misfortune on the journey or in California — and, like the farmer's circus elephant, an exotic sight, an unequaled experience, **the adventure of a lifetime**.

## Primary Sources: Voices on Hydraulic Mining

### Teacher Information — LearnCalifornia.org
Hydraulic mining was a California invention. The decision to curtail it was the point where agriculture became California's main industry. The "slickens" (sand and particulate matter suspended in water) filled streambeds and caused serious flooding in Central Valley towns. The flooding of Marysville and Sacramento in the 1860s was due in part to the filling of the riverbeds. Near Marysville, the riverbed was filled to the top of the banks. The rivers of the area had as much as 200 feet of tailings in their beds. The landscape was permanently changed.

The flumes and ditches for carrying water were eventually used for irrigation and later for generating electricity. PG&E bought many mining water ditch companies when it began making electricity near the turn of the century. Many of those ditches are still in use today.

### Explanation of Hydraulic Mining (Hutchings California Magazine, 1860)
> By far the most efficient system of mining yet known, for hill diggings, is the hydraulic; for the discovery of which California is indebted to Mr. Edward E. Matteson....The most efficient manner of washing down these banks is by undermining them near the bed rock, when large masses — frequently many tons in weight — "cave down" and not only break themselves to pieces by the fall, but unfortunately often bury the too venturesome miner beneath them.

### Effects of Hydraulic Mining 1 (William Brewer, October 1862)
> The amount of soil removed by hydraulic mining must be seen to be at all appreciated. Single claims will estimate it by the millions of tons; the "tailings" (refuse from the sluices) fill valleys, while the mud not only muddles the Sacramento River for more than four hundred miles of its course, but is slowly and surely filling up the Bay of San Francisco.

### Effects of Hydraulic Mining 2 (Titus Cronise, 1868)
> By no other means does man more completely change the face of nature than by this process of hydraulic mining. Hills melt away and disappear under its influence, every winter's freshets carrying to lower and yet lower points portions of the detritus, while whole valleys are filled with clean washed boulders of quartz and other rocks.

### Effects of Hydraulic Mining 3 (Samuel Bowles, 1868)
> Tornado, flood, earthquake and volcano combined could hardly make greater havoc, spread wider ruin and wreck, than are to be seen everywhere in the track of the larger gold-washing operations. None of the interior streams of California, though naturally pure as crystal, escape the change to a thick yellow mud from this cause, early in their progress from the hills.

### Effects of Hydraulic Mining 4 (Charles Nordhoff, 1872)
> [The Yuba River] was once, I am told by old residents, a swift and clear mountain torrent; it is now a turbid, and not rapid stream, whose bed has been raised by the washings of the miners not less than fifty feet above its level in 1849. It once contained trout, but now I imagine a catfish would die in it.

### Governor's Message of 1881 (Governor George Perkins, January 13, 1881)
Governor Perkins warned the California Legislature that if the debris problem was not addressed, the Sacramento Valley would "at no distant date, be rendered uninhabitable; that the rivers will cease to be navigable; that the Cities of Marysville, Sacramento, and Colusa...will be overwhelmed."

### Miner's Testimony (Lester Robinson, mine owner, 1878)
> The great evil of which complaint is made, to wit, filling our lower rivers, bays, and harbor, arises from and upon a much larger area than that operated upon by the hydraulic miners, and is very much more extensive and should not be charged upon the miners at all.

### Report of Legislature (Majority Report, Legislative Committee on Mining Debris, 1878)
> Fertile farms, immense orchards, blooming gardens, costly improvements, and homes happy with all the surroundings that embellish communities and enrich a State — all are engulfed in a common destruction, and their owners have gone out bankrupted from the merciless invasion of a foe that has left them penniless.

### Miner's Rights Article (Grass Valley Daily Union, January 1876)
> The farmers of the valleys....fail to remember that they located down on farms after the miners had acquired rights above the streams. Among the rights acquired by the miners was that of "dumpage" into the tributaries of the rivers.

### Farmers Statement (James Keyes, farmer, 1874)
> I have no objection to miners digging out all the gold they can find, but I don't want them to send the whole side of a hill down upon my ranch and bury me and all I have.

### Report of State Engineer 1 (William H. Hall, 1881)
> Orchards, gardens, fields, and dwellings were buried from sight, landmarks were lost, and the course of the devastating flood was marked out by broad commons of slimes and sands.

### Effects on Fish (Report of the Commissioners of the Fisheries, 1875)
> The Salmon certainly passed up these streams [Feather, Yuba and the American] for a few years after extensive mining was presented, but their spawning beds being covered by sediment, their eggs would not mature; and as the old fish died or were killed (no new ones taking their place), they became extinct in these streams.

### Nevada City Newspaper Opinion (Nevada City Transcript, November 1879)
> The idea of the city of Marysville bringing suits to abate the hydraulic mining companies on the Yuba and its branches as a nuisance is preposterous; to do so would simply be the confiscation of millions of dollars of property.

### Court Decision 1 — The Sawyer Decision (Judge Lorenzo Sawyer, January 7, 1884)
> The [defendant companies — North Bloomfield et al]....are perpetually enjoined and restrained from discharging or dumping into the Yuba river....any of the tailings, bowlders [sic], cobble stones, gravel, sand, debris or refuse matter.

### Court Decision 2 (State Supreme Court, November 1884)
> No person, natural or artificial, has the right, directly or indirectly, to cover his neighbor's land with mining.

### 1893 Statute (Chapter 223, Statutes of 1893)
> The business of hydraulic mining may be carried on within the State of California wherever and whenever the same can be carried on without material injury to the navigable streams, or lands adjacent thereto.

## Original Voices: On Native People

### The Gold Rush's Impact on Indigenous People

Although there had been limited contact between Indigenous nations and other cultures (such as Russian traders) in northern California prior to the Gold Rush, it wasn't until gold was discovered in May of 1849 on the banks of the Trinity River that miners from over the world began to flock to the North Coast. Tens of thousands of men from all over the United States, as well as from China, Russia, Mexico, Chile, Peru, Australia, and Great Britain came to the land.

They brought with them picks, shovels, mining pans, and guns. Miners found an estimated **3,950,000 troy ounces of gold** in all of California by 1852. At today's standards that amount of gold would be worth about $1.5 billion.

The "Little Giant" hydraulic monitor (1870) could force out water at a pressure equal to a 500-foot column of water. These machines were mostly financed by wealthy San Francisco investors and water companies. Mining had transformed very quickly from the independent, solitary miner to companies of men who worked for powerful interests within California.

### Bounty Hunting and Ethnic Cleansing

The act of paying for the death of another human being was practiced extensively by the California Legislature and the Federal Government during and after the Gold Rush. Towns and ranches throughout California also put out their own death warrants on indigenous people from 1848 to the 1870s.

Bounty hunting in northern California was often carried out by militias composed of local merchants and ranchers. In early 1863, Governor Leland Stanford and General Wright called for six companies of troops to be raised for action against Indians in the Humboldt District. Volunteers received $18 per month plus $100 bounty.

*An editorial from a newspaper of the time:* "The Indian war is defunct. The whole matter has been a cowardly farce, the threatening legions of Indians turning out to be but about 100, seeking refuge in a brush from the rowdies who, on the least occasion, delight in the sport of shooting them. As in all cases of this kind, the fault has been with the whites."

### Boarding Schools (Re-Education Centers)

The end of the Gold Rush era signaled a change in U.S. policy towards Native people. Instead of directly killing California indigenous people, reservations were created and children were taken — often by force — to far-away re-education centers. Children as young as four attended.

They were forced to cut their hair, give up their clothing. Girls learned to sew, clean house, and cook. Boys learned tanning, brick-making, and other manual chores. Speaking your native language resulted in severe punishment: whippings, isolation, withholding of food. Indian children were often farmed out as free labor to white settlers, and sometimes sold outright at auctions held by boarding school teachers.

*"You know how I was raised? In a boarding school, being slapped across the face, beaten for being an Indian, feeling ashamed of the color of my skin."* — Unidentified Yurok elder
