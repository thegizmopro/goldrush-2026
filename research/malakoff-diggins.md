# Malakoff Diggins State Historic Park

**Location:** 23579 North Bloomfield Road, Nevada City, CA 95959  
**Phone:** (530) 265-2740  
**Website:** www.parks.ca.gov | www.malakoffdiggins.org

> *At Malakoff Diggins, the world's largest hydraulic gold mine devastated the pristine landscape — leading to the first environmental law enacted in the nation.*

---

## Park History

### Native People

The park lies within the territory of the **Hill Nisenan**. Their territory once extended from the lower reaches of the Yuba, American, and Feather Rivers to the east bank of the Sacramento River and up to the 10,000-foot Sierra crest.

The Hill Nisenan lived in multi-family villages or extended-family hamlets, located below 3,000 feet elevation, in small valleys and open canyons. Families wintered in these villages, then spread to smaller camps at higher elevations from spring through fall to collect and hunt food.

- **1808** — First contact with Spanish, when General Gabriel Moraga passed through
- **1833** — Great malaria epidemic wiped out many of the Hill Nisenan
- **1848** — Gold Rush delivered the final blow: miners overran their territory, bringing disease and disrupting harvest patterns

The surviving Nisenan, known as the Southern Maidu in Nevada County, are seeking federal tribal recognition as they preserve their ancestral heritage.

---

### Miners Find Gold

As the Sierra Nevada eroded over eons, ancestral rivers deposited gold along their riverbeds. Gold panning quickly exhausted the readily available surface gold.

**Placer mining** began here in **1852** after a rich gold deposit was found in Humbug Creek, near the South Yuba River. Each placer miner staked claim to a 30- by 40-foot section of ground. They scooped and sieved gravel, dirt, and water into flat-bottomed pans, agitated the mixture (alluvial deposits), then poured off the water — the heavier gold remained as flakes or nuggets.

A town called **Humbug** sprang up. Miners devised more efficient methods:
- Long slanted sluice boxes or "rockers"
- Liquid mercury (quicksilver) added to create a gold-mercury amalgam that settled to the bottom while water, sand, and gravel ran off

---

### The Birth of Hydraulic Mining

In **1852**, French miner **Anthony Chabot** bypassed ditches and flumes by hooking a canvas hose directly to the water flow. When **Edward Mattison** added a nozzle to increase pressure, **hydraulic mining was born**. Discarded material (tailings) piled up on stream banks or washed downstream as silt.

In **1858**, Humbug expanded around Lake City, Relief Hill, and North Columbia — becoming **North Bloomfield Township**, with a sawmill, saloons, and breweries. Many Chinese immigrants labored in the mines and grew vegetables for residents.

---

### The North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company

In **1866**, French immigrant **Julius Poquillion** bought and consolidated 1,535 acres of abandoned claims. He convinced San Francisco financiers to invest, forming the **North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company**.

The company built:
- A massive flume and ditch system to carry water for ore washing
- Over **100 miles of canals and ditches**
- Capacity to work **100,000 tons of gravel per day**

**The Drainage Tunnel:**
- **7,847 feet** long, dug through bedrock from the Diggins pit to Humbug Creek
- Eight vent shafts sunk at 1,000-foot intervals
- Fifteen crews dug simultaneously — two per shaft, digging in opposite directions
- Completed **November 15, 1874** at a cost of **$275,574**

In **1876**, seven full-scale **Craig monitors** (powerful water cannons) were deployed to wash ore from mountainsides and uncover gold-laden quartz. The company spent **$3 million** on capital improvements — and collected only **$3 million in gold** before laws shut them down.

---

### The Sawyer Decision

By the 1850s, concerns emerged about hydraulic mining tailings. By the late 1860s, the debris problem was severe:
- Farms along the river were flooded and destroyed
- Silt reached San Francisco Bay, impairing navigation of the Sacramento River
- River channels closed to steamboat traffic

**1875** — The town of Marysville flooded catastrophically; surrounded by levees, floodwaters filled it like a bowl. Many residents lost property or lives.

Years of lawsuits, injunctions, appeals, and even property destruction followed. When the Rudyard Dam on the Yuba River failed in **1883**, miners suspected dynamite.

**January 7, 1884** — Judge Lorenzo Sawyer handed down his landmark decision in *Woodruff v. North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company*. In a **225-page document**, Sawyer issued a **permanent injunction** against dumping tailings into the Yuba River. California's hydraulic mining era ended — this ruling is considered the **first environmental law enacted in the nation**.

**Aftermath:**
- By 1900, North Bloomfield's population had shrunk by half
- Buildings stood empty and decaying
- WWI (1914): some buildings demolished for lumber
- Prohibition: saloons closed
- Depression era: former residents returned to empty buildings for free shelter
- WWII (1941): people left for war work; more buildings razed
- By 1950: fewer than 20 permanent residents

---

### Becoming a State Park

In the 1960s, Nevada County locals campaigned to preserve North Bloomfield's history. **Malakoff Diggins State Historic Park was created in 1965.** Visitors can see the remnants of the main Malakoff mine and five other hydraulically blasted claim sites.

## Natural History

### Geology and Vegetation

Sedimentary, igneous (mostly granite), metamorphic, and volcanic rock surround the mined areas. The pit's steep sides are fairly barren.

- Pit dimensions: ~6,800 feet long (SW to NE); 1,000–3,800 feet wide (N to S)
- Over 100 feet of soil has accumulated on the pit floor, transforming the raw U-shaped surface into a flat plane
- Native vegetation has grown back through once-exposed areas

About **3,200 forested acres** surround the pit at 2,500–4,000 feet elevation. The second-growth ponderosa pine forest also has incense cedar, black oak, white fir, and sugar pine.

Dominant shrubs: whiteleaf manzanita, ceanothus (buckbrush, deerbrush). Spectacular wildflowers bloom in spring.

### Wildlife

- **Nocturnal:** black bears, mountain lions, coyotes, bobcats
- **Daytime:** black-tailed deer
- **Birds:** dark-eyed junco, mountain chickadee, California quail, Steller's jay, black-throated gray warbler, mourning dove

### Climate

- Oct–Apr: 40–60 inches of rainfall on the western Sierra slope; snow common at higher elevations
- Spring, summer, and fall temperatures: high 50s to mid-80s

## Recreation

**Camping**
- Three miners' cabins in North Bloomfield (reservable)
- 30 family campsites at Chute Hill (tables, food lockers, fire grates)
- Group campsite for 9–60 people, max 18 vehicles
- Reservations: (800) 444-7275 or www.parks.ca.gov

**Swimming** — Blair Lake; small swimming section near picnic area. No lifeguard.

**Fishing** — Rainbow and brown trout in South Yuba River; black bass, bluegill, and rainbows in Blair Lake. Valid California fishing license required (age 16+).

**Hiking** — 20+ miles of scenic foothill trails (easy to strenuous). South Yuba Trail connects to park trail network.

**Programs & Events**
- Gold panning near China Garden
- Guided tours of historic town buildings (during museum hours)
- Kids' Fishing Derby in May
- Humbug Day in June
- Ice Cream Social in September
- Environmental Living programs for school groups

**Accessible:** Campsite #13 and restrooms are accessible. For updates: (916) 445-8949 or http://access.parks.ca.gov

## Visitor Rules

- Dogs on leash (max 6 ft) at all times; confined to tent or vehicle at night
- All natural and cultural features protected by law — do not disturb or remove
- **Poison oak** present throughout the park — contact causes serious rash
- **Ticks** may carry disease — wear long pants, check frequently
- **Black bears** — use bear-resistant metal lockers for all food and scented items
- Check in and pay day-use fees at park headquarters/museum in North Bloomfield
- Use only marked roads and trails — off-roading not permitted
