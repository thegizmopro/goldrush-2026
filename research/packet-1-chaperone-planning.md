## Wednesday: On Becoming Miners and Getting a Grubstake

### Activity Flow

**I. Upon arriving at Malakoff** (Susan in the picnic area): Walk back in time through the decades to 1849. At the moment this ends ("open your eyes") — the bandits come out.

**II. The Hold-Up**
- As part of the stick-up, it becomes clear the students have no gold and are "greenhorns"
- They also haven't paid for lodging or food
- The bandit offers them a "grubstake" — a loan. They'll go panning tomorrow and he's expecting repayment

**III. Students become miners**
- Encourage kids to invent their character: Where are they from? Why did they come to California? How did they get here? What are their hopes and fears?
- Kelsey and Susan distribute **color-coded bandanas** to mining groups

**IV. Hike to Ross Relles** (Susan leads) — character development on the trail. Adults share their stories too.

**V. Trading Post Game at Ross Relles**
- Grubstakes (miner's bucks) distributed
- Students pay for lodging and food, procure supplies, gamble, earn money, get taxed
- Trade miner's bucks for gold, pay for a mining claim, provide miner's name
- Make first payment for food and lodging

## The Town Hall Meeting

*The culmination of the first two days.*

Students dress up as one of the interest groups (determined mostly by which costume they choose):

### The Interest Groups

- **Central Valley farmers** — Downstream farms are being flooded with mud and debris from Malakoff. They are outraged.
- **The miners** — Most now work for the North Bloomfield Mining Company as hydraulic miners. Their livelihood depends on this enterprise.
- **North Bloomsfield merchants** — Like miners, they depend on the company town's economy.
- **Fishers** — Their livelihoods depend on clean rivers. They see firsthand what hydraulic mining is doing to the waterways.
- **The investors** — Mostly from San Francisco; have sunk a lot of money into this enterprise.

### About Judge Sawyer
Judge Lorenzo Sawyer was a real judge who in **1884** ruled that hydraulic mining at Malakoff was to be essentially halted — imposing strict laws regarding debris and closing all loopholes. His ruling was 225 pages, front-page news statewide, and is considered the **first environmental ruling in the United States**.

### Format
- One or two parents **coach** (not take over) the interest groups as they make opening statements, rebuttals, and closing statements
- Chris presides as Judge Sawyer
- After the ruling is announced, Susan brings kids back to 2025 for a debrief

### Additional Parent Roles
- Bailiff (to remove people out of line — parents are sometimes the worst offenders!)
- A character who shows up without a seat at the table (Chinese miner, Native person) who is told to leave
- A state engineer to testify about the wonders of hydraulic mining

## Campfire Characters

*The scene: Several newly arrived gold seekers gathered around a fire, swapping stories of their journeys to California (all came a different way), their hopes, fears, and hard luck stories.*

### Chinese Miner
- You only get the leftover mining claims on the creek
- You realized early on that your gold wasn't worth what others' was — nothing you can do about it
- Your grandmother is worried that if you die in California, your soul will wander here forever
- You started growing food for the miners in the "China garden" — you can make more money doing that. Some miners also come to you for health advice; you know herbs that cure sicknesses — but not the gold fever
- Some miners treat you very badly — tell you to go home, make fun of how you look and talk
- You plan to go home as soon as you make enough money

### Yankee Argonaut
- You came around the Horn — long and boring (six months at sea)
- Food was terrible but the boredom was worse
- 183 passengers total — all men except for one 12-year-old boy
- Captain Swain ("the wild bull of the sea") was in a race with another ship; won by risking all your lives through the deadly Strait of Magellan
- Your hopes and fears, plans, and how it's going so far

### One Who Took the Panama Route
- You came from the East Coast but took the short cut
- Tell how that went (crossing the Isthmus of Panama)
- Your hopes and fears, plans, and how it's going so far

### Pioneer Mom — Overland Trail
- Describe the hard journey across the country
- Part of a "wagon train" — 25 covered wagons; most people walked at ~3 miles per hour
- Each night: circle up wagons, sleep under the stars
- The worst part: getting wagons across rivers and deserts
- Five long months
- At the end, coming over the Sierra Nevada, people dumped precious possessions after carrying them all that way
- Maybe your husband is an engineer hired by the North Bloomfield Mining Company, and you have a job at the general store

### Gold Seeker from Mexico
- You came overland — long hot journey on foot (one mule to carry things)
- You hope to make money fast and go home
- You have cousins who own a beautiful rancho in Sonoma County — stayed with them before coming up here
- You're surprised to find Yankees are unfriendly to Mexican miners — they act like this land is already theirs, even though it all belonged to Mexico until very recently

## Some Local Color — North Bloomfield Characters

### Mary & John Blake
Owned and ran Ostrom Livery Stable. Horses were the main form of transportation — if you came to town, you left your horse here to be watched and cared for. You could also rent a freight wagon, fancy buggy, or another horse. In a pinch, you could sleep up in the hayloft.

### Willy Blood
A miner at Derbec. During a game of cards, he was shot through the heart. He was only 22 when he died in 1885. Will McKuan was sentenced to 10 years at Folsom Prison for the crime. Willy Blood is buried in the North Bloomfield Cemetery (look for a weathered wooden tombstone).

### Rush Dix Skidmore ("Skid")
Came to North Bloomfield in 1857 as a baker, later got into the saloon business. He also loaned money to local miners — all done verbally, but written in his black book. When a debt was past due, Skid would produce the black book. The miner always paid up. He knew all about US History and sponsored the town baseball team.

### Charlie Parkhurst
Drove stage for a living — and was a good driver. Famous for having ended the life of a much-feared bandit. Wore a patch over one eye, having lost it in a fight. What no one knew until Charlie died in 1880: **Charlie was a woman**. She dressed in men's clothes with a carf around her neck to hide the fact that she didn't have an Adam's apple. In **1868, Charlie was the first woman to vote** in the US, because everyone thought she was a man. She is buried in Aptos, California.

### Michael Cummings
Came to North Bloomfield from Ireland by way of the Isthmus of Panama. Worked for the North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company for over 30 years — a hard and dangerous job. His brother Edward owned the dance hall. Michael died when a blast of dynamite exploded prematurely, at age 57. His daughter Mary died of diphtheria at 8 years old.

### Lucky Dave Bowen
Worked down in the diggings. The hydraulic monitors shot water at such pressure that a hillside 500 feet away would crumble and wash away. The mine had a watchman on duty to watch for mudslides and cave-ins. One day, the cry came too late for Dave Bowen — a mudslide covered him completely. A quick count alerted the other men. A second slide happened before anything could be done. He got the name "Lucky Dave Bowen" because that second slide **uncovered his head**. He was alive. The other men dug him out with their bare hands, and he survived.

### Mr. Smith (Blacksmith)
Came to North Bloomfield seeking his fortune by panning gold. As a skilled blacksmith, he found he could make far more money making Penstock Pipes for the Malakoff Mines, helping them fit together. He also made horseshoes, pots, pans, and even hydraulic monitors.

### Old Man Eaves
Made his living by ground sluicing in the ravines and as a watchmaker. Lived in a cabin in the woods with a dozen or more cats. He loved to dream of flying like the birds. People told tales of how he once built himself a pair of wings and leapt from a high rock and nearly broke his neck.

### John Knotwell
Part-owner of the Smith-Knotwell Drug Store. Also a justice of the peace and county board supervisor. Could fix sore muscles, stomach aches, coughs, and rashes. Many remedies of the time contained morphine — a powerful painkiller that didn't treat the illness; you just couldn't feel the pain anymore. He died of pneumonia in 1901 at age 66. Buried in the N. Bloomfield Cemetery.

### Ah Coon
Lived in the Chinese section of town, the "Chinese Garden." A cross-looking fellow that most kids steered clear of. But get him down by the river and he was an expert panner — with a few motions, he easily held the pan in one hand and washed the gold.

### Doc DeMillieu ("Du mee yer")
A Frenchman, loyal to France but also to North Bloomfield. Always there when needed — pulling teeth, delivering babies, sewing up wounds. Traveled to patients by horse and buggy. Loved music and played clarinet in the town band.

### Pinky
Bookkeeper/paymaster for the Malakoff dam building crew at Sawmill Flat. He kept people on the payroll who didn't exist. He forged the boss' name on checks, then once a month went to Nevada City with buddies to cash the checks and have a weekend on the town.

### Benoit Fauchery
A French engineer who designed the Magenta Flume — an aqueduct that carried water across a deep canyon to feed the monitors in the Northern Mines. The Magenta Flume was 1,400 feet long and 160 feet tall, built from local timber in 1859. When completed, a great celebration was held: French and American flags raised, cannons fired salutes, and daring ladies and gentlemen walked across the flume.

### Hamilton Smith
Engineered the North Bloomfield tunnel to drain tailings from the Diggins pit into the South Yuba River. The tunnel was 300 feet deep and a mile and a half long. To finish quickly, they dug down in eight places and connected underground in a year and a half — part of the $3 million invested by San Francisco businessmen to build the largest hydraulic operation in the world.

### Old Franka
Used to be known as Black Frank because of his thick black beard, hair, and eyebrows. A quiet man who often began his answers with "wella suh" (well, sir). He had a lot of pride and wouldn't accept help for anything. He died in his sleep and is buried in the N. Bloomfield Cemetery.

### Jim Harrington
Watchman at the Malakoff mine, lived in the deserted Malakoff Hotel (about 20 rooms). Jim was a filthy man who never did dishes or cleaned house. When one room got too stuffed with junk, he just moved on into the next room.

## Cemetery Residents — North Bloomfield

*Some summaries of folks who lived in or near North Bloomfield, and how they lived and died. Some are in the cemetery and can shed light on how hard life was back then.*

- **Francis Blain** — French Canadian, came in 1854, obtained 60 acres for mining and agriculture. The North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company obtained half his property through legal procedures in 1874. That land is now Blair Lake and the Cummings house in town.

- **Cordelia, Elvena, and Florentina Gaus** — Children born in North Bloomfield who died from tuberculosis carried by the family's milk cow.

- **Jean Baptiste Ledu** — Came from France to mine in 1856; worked the Ledu Mine with his son Francis. He and his family are buried in the cemetery.

- **Charles Marr** — A native of Italy. When he died in North Bloomfield, over $4,000 was found hidden all over his home.

- **John Martens** — Came from Germany, made his way to North Bloomfield after living in Mississippi. Made his living by farming, blacksmithing, and mining. Killed in an accident at the Malakoff Mine.

- **Louis Miller** — Native of Germany, proprietor of a public house at Humbug. Refused to let Charles Davis take his daughter to the circus. A fight broke out. A gunshot was heard by the wife and daughter. Miller collapsed on the stairs. Davis was convicted of murder and sentenced to one year in San Quentin.

- **Arthur Monroe** — Died at 40 from injuries sustained when he broke his leg.

- **Caleb Nash** — Operated the general store from 1856 until it burned in 1869. Postmaster 1863–1870. His wife and daughters were seamstresses who gave piano lessons. Died at 55.

- **Michael Perfumo** — Disappeared on 1/10/1858 when he left to go from Humbug to Marysville. His injured dog was found, along with his hat, coat, and shoes with blood on them near the river and an old mine shaft.

- **Julius P. Poquillon** — Committed suicide on 6/10/1876 in San Francisco at 45 years of age. Built a hotel in Lake City in 1860 and formed the North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company.

- **Eddie Sherwood** — Died at 2 years old after drowning in a rain barrel behind the family home.

- **David Stokes** — Invented and patented the steerable monitor while employed by the North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company. Native of Switzerland; died at 39. Buried in the cemetery.
