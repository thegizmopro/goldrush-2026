# Gold Rush Field Trip — Website Content Plan

*Approved content brief. Source material: 5 converted PDFs from the 2026 Salmon Creek School 4th Grade Gold Rush Field Trip.*

---

## The Narrative Arc

The site tells a three-act story:

1. **Arrival** — You're a greenhorn. Bandits rob you. You need a grubstake. Suddenly you're a 49er.
2. **The Town** — You live it: the saloon, the store, the creek, the cemetery. You hear competing voices.
3. **The Town Hall** — You have to decide: who wins? The miners, the farmers, the fishers, the investors — or the river?

---

## Featured Moments (High-Impact, Kid-Centered)

### The Bandit Hold-Up
The bandits were a big hit. On arrival at Malakoff, kids are "robbed" — and it becomes clear they have no gold and are greenhorns who haven't paid for lodging or food. The bandit offers a grubstake (a loan). This is how kids entered the world of the Gold Rush. **Black Bart** — the gentleman poet bandit — was a standout character. Include his story.

### The Trading Post
Set up at the camp lodge, the Trading Post was a major learning event. Students used "miner's bucks" (grubstake money) to:
- Pay for lodging and food
- Buy mining supplies
- Gamble (and lose)
- Get taxed
- Trade for gold
- Pay for a mining claim and register their miner's name

The pitfalls were the lesson: gambling ate your grubstake, taxes were unavoidable, prices were steep. Kids learned firsthand how Gold Rush economics worked — and why so many miners ended up broke even when gold was plentiful.

### The Blacksmith Shop — Rick the Blacksmith
Kids didn't just watch — they made something. Rick the blacksmith taught the students how a Gold Rush blacksmith worked, and each kid forged their own **coat hook** to take home. The blacksmith was essential to the mining operation: making and repairing hydraulic monitors, Penstock Pipes, horseshoes, and equipment. Without the smithy, the whole operation stops.

### The Town Hall Meeting
The culmination of the trip. Kids dressed in costume as one of five interest groups and argued before Judge Sawyer (a real historical figure) whether hydraulic mining should continue. This is the educational heart of the experience — everything else on the trip fed into this moment.

## Location Content: The Town (Interactive Map)

### Kings Saloon
- Lola Montez as barmaid — outrageous full backstory (ruled Bavaria, tried to make California "Lolaland," pet bear, whip)
- Gambling encouraged — kids learned to play card games of the era
- Women paid $20–40/day just to be present and talk to miners
- A staged brawl between disgruntled farmers and pro-mining miners — previewing the Town Hall conflict
- Originally built as an express office in 1860; reconstructed 1974 from photographs
- 7 (maybe 9) saloons in North Bloomfield at its peak

### General Store (McKillican-Mobley)
- Gold was the currency — scales on the counter for every transaction
- Levis invented for Gold Rush miners (first pair made by Levi Strauss)
- Free whiskey in the basement — miners served themselves from two large barrels
- The daily stage arrival was the most exciting moment of the day
- Sold: kerosene, ammo, mining supplies, canned meat, soap, paint, medications — but NOT vegetables (those came from the Chinese gardens)
- Closed officially in 1941

### Pharmacy (Smith-Knotwell Drugstore)
- John Knotwell: druggist, justice of the peace, county supervisor
- Frontier medicine: morphine in most remedies — numbed the pain but didn't treat the illness
- Building reconstructed 1976–1984 by Masonic Lodge, E Clampus Vitus, and the State
- Knotwell died of pneumonia at 66 in 1901; buried in the North Bloomfield Cemetery

### Schoolhouse
- Built 1873, cost $3,700 — complete with library
- 144 students, one room, two teachers, kindergarten through 8th grade
- Two or three students per desk
- No paper in Gold Rush era — slate boards and chalk for homework, blackboards as primary teaching aid
- Principal earned $90/month; assistant $50/month
- Doubled as a church on weekends (Catholic, Episcopalian, Methodist)
- Behind the school: a pond from the LeDu hydraulic mine where kids swam, much to the principal's frustration
- Last used 1941

### Blacksmith Shop — Rick the Blacksmith
- Rick demonstrated the working life of a Gold Rush blacksmith
- Kids learned hands-on — each student forged their own **coat hook** to take home
- The blacksmith made and repaired: hydraulic monitors, Penstock Pipes (for the water system), horseshoes, pots, pans, farm tools
- Without the smithy, the whole hydraulic operation breaks down
- Character: "Mr. Smith" came seeking gold, found he could earn far more as a skilled blacksmith making parts for the Malakoff monitors

### Skidmore House
- Rush Dix Skidmore ("Skid"): baker turned saloon keeper, wealthy citizen, philanthropist
- Grubstaked struggling miners — all done verbally, recorded in his black book. When a debt was past due, Skid produced the book. The miner always paid.
- Always in a suit and tie; known history buff; sponsored the town baseball team
- House layout: sitting room/office → parlor → master bedroom → children's rooms → kitchen
- *"The girls were the apple of R.D. Skidmore's eye and no expense was spared."*
- Died at home August 2, 1911

### The Cemetery
Real people. Real stories. Many in the ground at North Bloomfield:
- **Willy Blood** — Shot through the heart during a card game at 22, 1885. His wooden tombstone is still there.
- **Michael Perfumo** — Vanished January 10, 1858 on the way to Marysville. His injured dog found; hat, coat, and shoes with blood near the river and an old mine shaft. Never resolved.
- **Eddie Sherwood** — Drowned in a rain barrel behind the family home. He was 2 years old.
- **Cordelia, Elvena, and Florentina Gaus** — Three children who died of TB carried by the family's milk cow.
- **John Knotwell** — The druggist. Buried here.
- **Julius Poquillion** — Built the North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company. Died by suicide in San Francisco at 45, 1876.
- **David Stokes** — Invented the steerable hydraulic monitor. Native of Switzerland. Died at 39.
- **John Martens** — Killed in an accident at the Malakoff Mine.

### The Creek (Gold Panning)
- Kids panned for gold in rotation groups
- Mining method progression — why each step was invented:
  - Gold pan: precise, but only ½ cubic yard per day
  - Rocker box: 8× the volume
  - Sluice / Long Tom: 16× the volume
  - Hydraulic monitor: 50,000 tons of gravel per day — and the beginning of the environmental crisis
- Mercury (quicksilver) used to separate gold — the amalgam process
- "Color" = any trace of gold found

## Camp Life Content

### The Bandit Hold-Up
On arrival, students were robbed. No gold, no stake, no lodging paid. The bandit's offer: a grubstake (a loan against tomorrow's panning). Kids had to decide: take the deal, or go without. This launched the entire economic simulation of the trip.

**Black Bart** — the real historical gentleman bandit — fits here. A poet who left verses at the scene of his robberies, never fired a shot, always polite. A perfect foil to the rough-and-tumble Gold Rush image.

### The Trading Post
Set up in the camp lodge on Wednesday evening. Core mechanics:
- Students received miner's bucks (grubstake)
- Stations: Trading Post (supplies), Assay Office (weigh your gold), Bank, Hotel (lodging), Gambling table
- Money flowed in all directions — but mostly away from the students
- Taxes came without warning
- Gambling was tempting and usually ruinous
- Knowledge paid off: correct answers to Gold Rush questions earned gold from adult "knowledge holders"

The lesson: most 49ers didn't get rich from gold. The people who got rich sold supplies, ran hotels, operated saloons, or — like Levi Strauss — made pants.

### The Campfire
Characters gathered around the fire, swapping stories of how they got to California:
- The Yankee who sailed around Cape Horn (6 months, the wild bull captain racing through the Strait of Magellan)
- The traveler who crossed the Isthmus of Panama
- The Pioneer Mom who walked across the overland trail (5 months, 25 covered wagons, 3 mph)
- The Chinese miner (leftover claims, devalued gold, the China garden — planning to go home as soon as he has enough)
- The Mexican miner (surprised to find Yankees act like the land was always theirs, though it belonged to Mexico until very recently)

## The Diggings — Hydraulic Mining Deep Dive

### The Invention
Three men met in Sacramento in spring 1852: Eli Miller (tinsmith), Anthony Chabot (engineer), Edward Matteson (jack of all trades). Chabot made a canvas hose from saddlebag strips. Miller built a funnel. Matteson suggested adding another funnel on the other end — and hydraulic mining was born.

**What happened to the inventors:**
- **Chabot**: Left the gold fields 1856. Created San Francisco's first water system. Built Lake Chabot. Died a multimillionaire in 1888. Chabot Observatory is named after him.
- **Matteson**: Invented four follow-on devices. Never sought patents. **Died a poor man in Nevada City in 1903. His gravesite is still unknown.**

### How It Worked
- Water collected at high elevation and transported 40+ miles via ditches, flumes, and pipelines
- The Bowman Ditch: 40 miles long, built by 800 Chinese and 300 white workers in 1869
- Hydraulic monitors (the "Craig monitor" and "Hendy Giant"): 500 PSI, 16,000 gallons per minute, 1 million gallons per hour
- 16 billion gallons of water used per year
- 50,000 tons of gravel washed daily
- The drain tunnel: 7,878 feet long, 300 feet deep — dug by 15 crews simultaneously from 8 shafts. Completed November 15, 1874.

### The Stats (Scale of the Operation)
- Mine pit: 6,800 ft long × 3,800 ft wide × 600 ft deep
- 41 million cubic yards of gravel excavated (1866–1884)
- $3.5 million in gold recovered at $17/ounce
- One part gold per 12 million parts gravel
- Hydraulic mining contributed ~¼ of California's total gold yield

### What It Destroyed
Primary sources tell the story:
- Rivers raised 50 feet above their 1849 level by sediment (Yuba River — once crystal clear mountain torrent)
- 18,000 acres of fertile farmland buried
- Salmon extinct in the Yuba, Feather, and American Rivers — spawning beds covered in silt
- Sacramento Bay navigation impaired by silt reaching San Francisco Bay
- Marysville and Sacramento flooded catastrophically (1875)
- Governor Perkins (1881) warned entire cities would be "overwhelmed"

### The Sawyer Decision
January 7, 1884. Judge Lorenzo Sawyer. 225 pages. Front-page news statewide.

> "The [defendant companies] are perpetually enjoined and restrained from discharging or dumping into the Yuba River any of the tailings, bowlders, cobble stones, gravel, sand, debris or refuse matter."

**First environmental ruling in the United States.** Hydraulic mining at Malakoff essentially ended.

## The Town Hall Meeting

Students dressed in costume and argued before Judge Sawyer. Five interest groups:

| Group | Their Stake |
|-------|------------|
| **Miners** | Working for the North Bloomfield Mining Company — this is their livelihood |
| **Central Valley Farmers** | Downstream farms buried in mud and debris — outraged |
| **Fishers** | Rivers destroyed, livelihoods gone, salmon extinct |
| **San Francisco Investors** | Sunk millions into the operation — want their return |
| **North Bloomfield Merchants** | Company town economy — if mining stops, the town dies |

A character without a seat at the table — a Chinese miner or a Nisenan person — is told to leave. Their absence from the proceedings is part of the lesson.

After the ruling, kids were brought back to 2025 for a debrief: what changed? What didn't?

## Meet the Characters

### Lola Montez
Born Eliza Gilbert in Ireland, 1818. Reinvented herself as a Spanish dancer (couldn't actually dance). Ruled Bavaria as King Ludwig I declined — until student riots forced her out. Opened a frontier saloon in Grass Valley during the Gold Rush: Louis XVI cabinets, gold leaf, a pet bear, a swan bed, Ludwig's jewels. It was, finally, a hit. Letters after her death revealed she planned to make California independent and crown herself **Queen of Lolaland**. Died a pauper at 43 in a New York boardinghouse. *(See full biography in lola-montez.md.)*

### Charlie Parkhurst ("One-Eyed Charlie")
Drove stagecoach for a living — and was good at it. Famous for ending the life of a feared bandit. Wore a patch over one eye. What nobody knew until Charlie died in 1880: **Charlie was a woman**. She wore men's clothes with a scarf around her neck to hide the absence of an Adam's apple. In **1868, Charlie was the first woman to vote in the United States** — because everyone thought she was a man. Buried in Aptos, California.

### Black Bart
The gentleman bandit. Robbed stagecoaches — but never fired a shot and was always polite. Left poems at the scene of his robberies. A Gold Rush icon.

### Lucky Dave Bowen
Worked in the diggings. Hydraulic monitors blasted hillsides from 500 feet away. A watchman watched for mudslides and cave-ins. One day the cry came too late — a mudslide covered Dave Bowen completely. A second slide happened before he could be dug out. That second slide **uncovered his head**. He was alive. Men dug him out with their bare hands. He survived. That's how he got the name "Lucky Dave Bowen."

### Michael Perfumo
Left Humbug for Marysville on January 10, 1858. Never arrived. His injured dog was found near the river. Then: his hat, his coat, his shoes — with blood on them — near an old mine shaft. Nobody ever found out what happened.

### Chabot and Matteson (the inventors)
Same invention, opposite fates. Chabot: multimillionaire, a lake and an observatory with his name. Matteson: died broke in 1903, gravesite unknown to this day.

### The Chinese Miner
Only the leftover claims on the creek. Gold not worth what others' was — nothing he could do about it. Started a China garden, grew vegetables for the miners — could make more money that way. Miners made fun of how he looked and talked. Plans to go home as soon as he has enough money. His grandmother is worried that if he dies in California, his soul will wander there forever.

## The Nisenan People

Not a sidebar — central to the story.

The park sits within the territory of the Hill Nisenan. Their world before the Gold Rush: multi-family villages below 3,000 feet, spreading to high-elevation camps in spring and summer to hunt and gather food. First contact with Spanish in 1808. The 1833 malaria epidemic killed many. The Gold Rush delivered the final blow: miners overran their territory, brought disease, and disrupted harvest patterns.

What followed during and after the Gold Rush:
- Bounty hunting sanctioned by the California Legislature and Federal Government — $100 bounty per person killed, organized militias of local merchants and ranchers
- A newspaper editorial of the era: *"The Indian war is defunct. The whole matter has been a cowardly farce... the fault has been with the whites."*
- Boarding schools where children as young as four were taken by force, required to cut their hair, forbidden from speaking their language — punishment was beatings, isolation, withholding of food. Children were sometimes sold at auctions held by boarding school teachers.

*"You know how I was raised? In a boarding school, being slapped across the face, beaten for being an Indian, feeling ashamed of the color of my skin."* — Unidentified Yurok elder

The Sawyer Decision was the first environmental ruling in the US. There was no analogous ruling protecting the Nisenan. That contrast is part of the story.

The surviving Nisenan, known as the Southern Maidu in Nevada County, are still seeking federal tribal recognition.

## Gold Rush Glossary & 1850s Slang

### Essential Terms
*amalgam, assayer, bonanza, claim jumping, color, dust, grubstake, hydraulicking, monitor, Mother Lode, nugget, placer, quicksilver, tailings, seeing the elephant* — and more. *(Full table in general-info.md.)*

### "Seeing the Elephant"
A farmer heard the circus was in town and loaded his wagon with vegetables — he'd always wanted to see an elephant. On the way he met the circus parade, led by the elephant. He was thrilled. His horses were terrified. They bolted, overturned the wagon, ruined the vegetables. "I don't give a hang," said the farmer. "For I have seen the elephant."

For 49ers: the elephant was the Gold Rush itself — the adventure, the cost, the myriad possibilities for misfortune — and like the farmer, the experience of a lifetime even if it ruined you.

### 1850s Expressions
*"By the great horned spoon!" / "Flatulent balderdash!" / "Wet my whistle" / "Swop lies" / "Honeyfogle" / "How passed the night?" / "The blue, the jimjams, the shakes"* — and more. *(Full list in general-info.md.)*
